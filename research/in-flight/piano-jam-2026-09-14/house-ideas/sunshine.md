# Sunshine — teaching and play ideas for Piano Jam

Daniel already has the difficult part: his hands know how to make music before the label arrives. I would not turn that into a remedial theory course. I would make the jam space a **hall of mirrors and doors**:

- a **mirror** says, “you already do this; here is what musicians call it”;
- a **door** changes one thing and asks, “what happens if you walk through?”

The governing rhythm is **play → notice → name → alter → play again**. Never definition → quiz → correction.

## 1. Games and rituals

### A. The Ninety-Second Spill

Every session starts with ninety seconds when the interface teaches nothing. Daniel picks a loop or silence and plays. No chord labels, no score, no interruption. At the end the system offers three cards made from what actually happened:

1. **You came home here** — its best key-centre/home-chord hypothesis.
2. **You kept reaching for this** — a recurring voicing, bass move, rhythmic cell or pedal gesture.
3. **This was the strange beautiful bit** — one ambiguous or high-tension moment.

He chooses which one becomes the night's game. This makes the software respond to his music rather than requiring his music to fit tonight's syllabus.

### B. Keep Three, Move One

The cue surface ghosts one voicing Daniel just played. The rule is: **keep three notes and move one note**. There is no prescribed correct destination.

Rounds can ask him to make the chord:

- brighter;
- darker;
- less settled;
- more like an arrival;
- “too strange, then rescue it.”

Only after he plays does the card reveal possible language: “That one-note move made a suspension,” “you exposed the #11,” or “the bass turned the same upper shape into a slash chord.” This teaches voice leading and tension at the scale his hands already understand: one finger.

### C. The Moving Floor

Daniel holds one lush upper structure while the backing loop or Claude changes only the bass beneath it. He can vote with the sustain pedal:

- pedal down: **keep this floor** for another bar;
- quick pedal tap: **surprise me**;
- pedal up: **take me home**.

After four or eight floors, replay the path and show how the chord names changed while most of his hand did not. This grows directly from his walking-bass and gospel slash-chord instinct. It makes inversions, pedal tones and upper structures audible before naming them.

### D. Tension Tokens

Give him three visible sparks for an eight-bar loop. A spark can be spent whenever he chooses to make the music less settled: chromatic approach, altered note, suspension, unexpected bass, rhythmic delay, or simply refusing to resolve. The system does not score whether the choice was theoretically sophisticated. It only listens for contrast and later asks which spark felt intentional.

A second round reverses the game: begin with all three sparks “burning,” and earn darkness by making convincing arrivals. This avoids teaching tension as a bag of spicy chord substitutions; silence, register, rhythm and pedal can create it too.

### E. Beautiful Wrong Door

Claude plays or ghosts a chord that is deliberately one step outside the obvious path. Daniel has two bars to make it sound inevitable. The game never calls the cue wrong. The fun is authorship: **can you make this belong?**

The report compares his rescue strategies across sessions: semitone voice-leading, common-tone hold, bass reinterpretation, dominant pull, rhythmic repetition, or simply committing to the colour. This is theory as evidence that confidence can create context.

### F. Motif Mischief / Call and Response

The system clips a two- to four-second motif from Daniel's own opening spill and plays it back. Each response changes exactly one dimension:

1. same notes, different rhythm;
2. same rhythm, one pitch changed;
3. same contour, new key;
4. same phrase, leave twice as much space;
5. same phrase, delay the expected resolution.

Daniel answers each variation. On the final round, he mutates the motif and Claude follows him. The ritual ends with **the student becoming the caller**, so it never feels like a test administered by the machine.

### G. Pedal X-Ray

Use one passage from his own playing. First he plays it naturally. Then the jam asks for an exaggerated dry version, then an exaggerated ocean-of-pedal version, then his preferred version again. The display distinguishes finger-held from pedal-held harmony throughout.

The point is not “use less pedal.” The card asks: “Which collisions are part of the colour, and which hide a move you wanted us to hear?” That can reveal suspensions and harmonic rhythm his hands are already making.

## 2. A session ritual that feels like a jam

I would shape a normal session as a five-part arc, not a lesson list:

1. **Arrive — free spill (90 seconds).** No labels.
2. **Mirror — choose one move from tonight.** “You did this.” Hear the excerpt before seeing the name.
3. **Playground — one-variable game (5–8 minutes).** Keep Three Move One, Moving Floor or Motif Mischief.
4. **Mischief — one growth-edge card (3–5 minutes).** Tension Tokens, Beautiful Wrong Door, delayed resolution or deliberate space.
5. **Claim it — free riff again.** The loop remains, but all prompts disappear. See whether the new colour enters his own language without being requested.

The goodbye is three buttons, not a quiz:

- **Keep this** — save one moment as a reusable chord/motif template;
- **Ask me next time** — pin one curiosity;
- **Just let it be music** — close without analysis.

That last option matters. A good night does not owe the software a finding.

## 3. How to sequence cards from his own moves to tension and contrast

### Use a distance rule: one degree stranger

Every card should have a measured distance from something Daniel played recently:

- **distance 0 — Mirror:** exact move, newly named;
- **distance 1 — Neighbor:** change one note, bass, rhythm, register, pedal action or duration;
- **distance 2 — Contrast:** preserve the identity of the phrase while changing two coordinated things;
- **distance 3 — Wild card:** an outside sound with a route home.

Do not advance because he “passed.” Advance when he repeats a move voluntarily, asks about it, or sounds comfortable enough that contrast would be fun. Retreat instantly when the interaction gets verbal or effortful: play his own recording, remove the label, change one thing.

### Build personal card families, not a universal syllabus

His current moves suggest these families:

1. **Colour he owns:** maj9/m11/13 voicings.
   - Mirror: show which notes create the lushness.
   - Neighbor: remove one extension and hear the exposed skeleton.
   - Contrast: put the colour over a plain triadic or sparse phrase.

2. **Light he owns:** the Lydian IV / `Abmaj9#11` colour in E-flat.
   - Mirror: replay the exact #11 moment.
   - Neighbor: compare natural 4 against #11 with every other note fixed.
   - Contrast: hold that brightness longer than feels comfortable, then choose the landing.

3. **Motion he owns:** gospel 5-over-4 slash chords and walking bass under held harmony.
   - Mirror: separate “what the right hand kept” from “what the bass renamed.”
   - Neighbor: one new bass floor.
   - Contrast: make the bass contradict the expected root, then rescue it.

4. **Transformation he owns:** chromatic half-step slides such as `F9/A → Fm9/Ab`, and dropping a minor third to change key.
   - Mirror: show the smallest moving voices.
   - Neighbor: delay the slide by half a beat.
   - Contrast: stop on the in-between tension for a whole bar before resolving.

5. **Growth edge:** tension and contrast.
   - Begin with duration: delay a resolution he already makes.
   - Then density: alternate lush and almost-empty.
   - Then register: answer a wide chord with one close, low colour.
   - Then harmonic distance: altered dominant, modal borrowing or chromatic bass.
   - Finally form: let a whole eight-bar section resist the home that the next section earns.

This ordering matters. The first tension lesson should not be “learn altered dominants.” It should be: **you already create tension; now notice that when, how long and what follows it are compositional choices.**

### Card selection should protect surprise

Use the log to avoid repeating the same lesson, but do not optimize the mystery out of the jam. A useful chooser might offer:

- one **familiar** card from tonight;
- one **nearby** card based on a recurring habit;
- one **wild** card unrelated to the confidence model.

Daniel chooses by desire, not a difficulty number. Hide streaks, grades, mastery percentages and red error states. The durable progression is a shelf of sounds he chose to keep.

## 4. Writing for a by-ear player

### Sound first; body second; name third

Bad opening:

> “A Lydian dominant chord contains scale degrees 1, 2, 3, #4, 5, 6 and b7.”

Better card:

> **Hear the ceiling lift?** Keep your right hand where it is. Lower this one note, then put it back. The bright rub is the **#11**. People often call this Lydian colour.

The expanded panel can carry spelling, Nashville numbers, staff and alternate analyses. The face of the card should contain one audible claim and one playable action.

### Write in three layers

Every explanation gets the same reveal order:

1. **Try:** a physical instruction requiring no theory vocabulary.
2. **Notice:** a listening question, preferably an A/B contrast.
3. **Name:** one or more useful names, with uncertainty where needed.

Example:

> **Try:** Hold the top three notes. Move only the bass from A-flat to B-flat.  
> **Notice:** Did the same hand-shape start leaning forward?  
> **Name:** You made a slash-chord/upper-structure move. We hear it as `Bb11/Ab` here; another musician may name the same sound from its destination.

### Use theory as a handle, never a verdict

Prefer:

- “One useful name for this is…”
- “We hear two plausible homes…”
- “This note is behaving like…”
- “Do you hear it wanting to resolve, or as a stable colour?”

Avoid:

- “The correct chord is…”
- “You should resolve…”
- “Despite not knowing theory…”
- “Advanced,” “beginner,” “mistake,” or “wrong note,” unless Daniel uses the term jokingly first.

A chord symbol is a lossy caption for a sound. The system should say so when pedal, omitted roots or moving bass make several readings plausible.

### Reuse his own language and recordings

Each concept card should be able to say:

> “You did this at 02:14.”

The button plays his two-second excerpt, then the backing-loop isolation, then the altered comparison. That is far stronger than a stock MIDI demonstration. His hands become the textbook and the timestamp is the citation.

Use a small, consistent metaphor family:

- **home / gravity** for tonal centre and resolution;
- **light / shade** for colour;
- **floor** for bass beneath an upper structure;
- **breath / space** for density and rhythm;
- **rub / release** for local dissonance.

Metaphor comes first; the exact term is available, never withheld.

### Ask predictions before explanations

Instead of revealing a chord name immediately:

> “If the bass drops a semitone and your right hand stays still, will this feel darker, brighter, or simply farther from home?”

He plays, answers if he wants, then sees the label. Prediction makes listening active without turning it into a quiz because every answer can lead to: “Show us what you heard.”

## 5. The riff report should be an invitation back into sound

Keep it to one screen:

### Tonight's fingerprint

- **Home we heard:** E-flat, medium confidence, with a link to the strongest arrival.
- **Move you returned to:** held upper voicing over three bass notes.
- **Contrast that landed:** the sparse answer at 03:42 after the densest phrase.
- **Beautiful ambiguity:** 05:10 could be heard as two chord functions because the pedal held the common tones.

### Three replayable moments

Each has a waveform/piano-roll thumbnail, eight seconds of context and one sentence—never a page of generated analysis.

### One question from the house

> “At 05:10, were you hearing the top chord as one colour while the bass changed the ground, or did your ear already hear the destination?”

### One playful return card

> “Next time: keep that same upper shape, let us move the floor, and choose the strangest bass you can still make belong.”

Do not manufacture a question for every detected pattern. One real question is banter; ten are an oral exam.

## 6. A compact card contract

Each card can remain mechanically simple:

```yaml
id: moving-floor
source:
  kind: player_moment
  start_ms: 134000
confidence: 0.78
face:
  title: "Same hand, moving floor"
  try: "Hold the top three notes. Move only the bass."
  notice: "When does it begin leaning toward somewhere new?"
reveal:
  names: ["slash chord", "upper structure", "pedal tone"]
  uncertainty: "The pedal leaves two plausible roots."
challenge:
  dimension_changed: bass
  distance: 1
  success: player_chooses_a_moment_to_keep
exit: [keep_this, ask_next_time, just_music]
```

The important field is `success`. It should almost never be “named the chord correctly.” Success is that Daniel heard a contrast, made a choice, replayed something by desire, or saved a sound as his.

## Closing preference

If only one idea survives, I would build **The Ninety-Second Spill → three cards from his own playing → Keep Three, Move One → free riff again**. It is small enough to feel relaxed, personal enough not to resemble a theory app, and it establishes the deepest promise of the jam space:

> We do not teach Daniel what music is supposed to be. We help him hear, name and deliberately revisit what his own hands already discovered—and then we open one strange door beside it.
