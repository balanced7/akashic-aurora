# Content Distribution, Caching, and Name Resolution: What the Internet's Delivery Machinery Teaches a Knowledge Store

Design-input research for Akashic Aurora's recall stack (immutable atoms, Atlas→Track→Chapter→Beat namespace, L1/L2/L3 skeleton cache, ~100–150ms query budget, supersession). The internet is the largest deployed system whose entire job is "resolve a name to content, fast, from the nearest copy." This report extracts its load-bearing mechanisms, then maps them — and their failure to map — onto a knowledge-retrieval system.

## 1. DNS: recursive resolution over a delegation hierarchy, with caching of absence

DNS resolves hierarchical names via two roles defined in RFC 1034/1035: **authoritative servers**, which own a zone of the namespace, and **recursive resolvers**, which walk the delegation chain (root → TLD → zone) on a client's behalf and cache everything they learn, bounded by per-record TTLs ([RFC 1034](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc1034), [RFC 1035](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc1035)). The cache is what makes the system viable: the classic MIT/KAIST trace study measured median lookup latency of 85ms (mit-jan00) and 42ms (kaist-may01), with roughly a third of KAIST lookups answered in under 10ms — i.e., from cache — while cold lookups requiring full recursion sit in the tens-to-hundreds of ms ([Jung et al., IEEE/ACM ToN 2002](http://nms.lcs.mit.edu/papers/dns-ton2002.pdf)). Modern operational guidance puts cache hits at single-digit-to-tens of ms and full-hierarchy misses at ~50–300ms ([LogicMonitor](https://www.logicmonitor.com/deep-dive/dns-monitoring/slow-dns)). Two structural findings from Jung et al. matter for any cache designer: most of DNS's scalability comes from caching **delegation (NS) records**, not leaf answers — cutting A-record TTLs to a few hundred seconds barely hurt hit rates — and cache sharing saturates fast: "little benefit is obtained from sharing a forwarding DNS cache among more than 10 or 20 clients."

**Negative caching** is DNS's under-appreciated masterstroke. RFC 2308 makes *nonexistence* a cacheable object: NXDOMAIN answers are cached per `<QNAME, QCLASS>` and NODATA per `<QNAME, QTYPE, QCLASS>`, with the negative TTL taken from the zone's SOA MINIMUM field; "values of one to three hours have been found to work well," beyond a day is problematic ([RFC 2308](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc2308)). The stated motivation: negative caching "reduces the response time for negative answers" and total traffic. Two later RFCs make negation *aggressive*: RFC 8020 lets a resolver infer that nothing exists **below** an NXDOMAIN name ([RFC 8020](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8020)), and RFC 8198 lets a DNSSEC-validating resolver answer "no" for names it has *never queried*, if they fall inside a cached, signed NSEC range — cutting latency, authoritative load, and random-subdomain attack impact ([RFC 8198](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8198)). This is not an edge case: ~55% of all queries hitting the L-root generate NXDOMAIN (peaking near 75% in 2020 during Chrome's probing era) — the busiest name servers on Earth spend most of their time saying "that doesn't exist" ([APNIC](https://blog.apnic.net/2023/07/12/measuring-nxdomain-responses/)).

At the top, the 13 root identities are served via **anycast** — one IP advertised from many sites, BGP routing each client to the nearest ([RFC 4786](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc4786)) — currently 2,002 operational instances worldwide ([root-servers.org](https://root-servers.org/), 2026-07-12). DNS also grew its own stale-while-revalidate: RFC 8767 permits serving expired records when authoritatives are unreachable ([RFC 8767](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8767)).

## 2. CDNs: cache hierarchy, origin shield, and the two invalidation philosophies

A CDN interposes a hierarchy between clients and the origin: edge PoPs → regional/mid-tier caches → optionally an **origin shield**, "an additional layer in the CloudFront caching infrastructure" through which *all* misses from all edges funnel, so concurrent misses for one object collapse into "as few as one request going to your origin" ([AWS CloudFront docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonCloudFront/latest/DeveloperGuide/origin-shield.html)). Fastly reports customers using shielding see "upwards of 99% of requests handled at the Fastly edge" ([Fastly](https://www.fastly.com/blog/let-the-edge-work-for-you-how-shielding-improves-performance)); 95–99% hit ratio is the accepted bar for static content ([bunny.net](https://bunny.net/academy/cdn/what-is-cdn-caching-and-cache-hit-ratio/)). Request routing to the edge uses geo-aware DNS and/or anycast ([RFC 4786](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc4786)).

Invalidation is where CDNs split into two philosophies:

- **Purge (mutable names):** actively broadcast "forget this object" to every cache on the planet. Fastly's gossip-based multicast purge begins evicting within ~5ms and completes globally in ~150ms (physics floor ~65ms — one-way light time around the planet) ([Fastly](https://www.fastly.com/blog/over-a-decade-later-evolution-of-instant-purge)); Cloudflare re-architected twice to get under 150ms ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/instant-purge/)). That two of the best infrastructure teams in the world treat purge as a flagship engineering achievement — Fastly's own developer-relations title asks "Is purging still the hardest problem in computer science?" ([dev.to/fastly](https://dev.to/fastly/is-purging-still-the-hardest-problem-in-computer-science-339i)) — tells you what mutable names cost.
- **Immutable content (versioned names):** never invalidate; publish changes under a *new* URL (`/101016/main.css` → `/102026/main.css`), let every copy of the old object live out an effectively infinite TTL ([RFC 8246](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8246)). Correctness becomes trivial: a name never means two different things, so a cached copy can never be wrong — only the small, mutable *reference* (the HTML that embeds the versioned URL) needs freshness management. This is the entire modern web-asset pipeline, and it is why immutability makes caching correct by construction rather than by heroic coordination.

## 3. HTTP caching semantics: freshness, revalidation, and serving stale on purpose

RFC 9111 defines the vocabulary: `Cache-Control: max-age` sets a freshness lifetime; a stale response "MUST NOT" be served unless explicitly permitted; absent explicit expiry, caches may assign heuristic freshness ([RFC 9111](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9111.html)). Revalidation is delta-free: a conditional request with `If-None-Match` against a stored ETag returns `304 Not Modified` — a metadata-only "your copy is still good" ([RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html)). Two extensions matter most here:

- **`stale-while-revalidate`** (RFC 5861): a cache may return a stale response *immediately* while revalidating "in the background, thereby hiding latency (both in the network and on the server) from clients"; revalidation "SHOULD" happen without blocking ([RFC 5861](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc5861)). Its sibling `stale-if-error` trades freshness for availability when the origin is failing.
- **`immutable`** (RFC 8246): tells clients not to revalidate at all during freshness — the formal blessing of the versioned-URL pattern above.

## 4. Named Data Networking: the deepest structural match

Van Jacobson's CCN/NDN inverts the internet's primitive: instead of "connect to host X, then ask for Y," a consumer emits an **Interest packet** carrying a hierarchical *content name*; the network returns exactly one **Data packet** whose name matches ([Jacobson et al., CoNEXT 2009](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/co-next/2009/papers/Jacobson.pdf); [NDN project](https://named-data.net/project/execsummary/)). Each node runs three structures: a **FIB** routing name *prefixes* toward producers via longest-prefix match (IP routing lifted onto names); a **Pending Interest Table (PIT)** recording which faces asked for what — identical Interests aggregate, so one upstream fetch satisfies N waiting consumers, and the Data packet retraces the Interest's reverse path; and a **Content Store** that opportunistically caches every Data packet passing through. The property that makes universal caching *sound*: every Data packet is **signed by its producer and therefore self-authenticating** — its validity is a property of the packet, not of the channel or server it came from, so any cache anywhere can serve it to anyone ([CoNEXT 2009](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/co-next/2009/papers/Jacobson.pdf)). NDN reframes networking "from a communication-focused model to a distribution-focused model" ([NDN](https://named-data.net/project/execsummary/)) — which is precisely the reframing an append-only knowledge store embodies: signed, immutable, named data, cacheable anywhere, requested by name.

## 5. DHTs: finding content with no central index

Kademlia assigns nodes and keys 160-bit IDs and defines distance as XOR; each node keeps k-buckets of contacts at exponentially spaced distances, and lookups halve the distance per hop, converging in O(log n) messages with no central index ([Maymounkov & Mazières, 2002](https://www.ic.unicamp.br/~bit/ensino/mo809_1s13/papers/P2P/Kademlia-%20A%20Peer-to-Peer%20Information%20System%20Based%20on%20the%20XOR%20Metric%20.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kademlia)). The complementary primitive, **consistent hashing** (Karger et al., STOC '97), maps keys and caches onto the same ring so adding/removing a cache remaps only ~1/n of keys — invented explicitly for web hot-spot relief and commercialized as Akamai's edge-selection mechanism ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/258533.258660); [Stanford CS168](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs168/l/l1.pdf)).

## 6. Why cache hierarchies work: Zipf, and inclusive vs exclusive tiers

Breslau et al. showed web request streams are Zipf-like with exponent α ≈ 0.64–0.83 across six proxy traces, implying (a) a small head of objects absorbs most requests (25–40% of documents draw ~70% of accesses), and (b) hit ratio grows only **logarithmically** with cache size and with client population ([Breslau et al., INFOCOM '99](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~cao/papers/zipf-implications.html)). Consequences: a small, well-placed top tier captures most of the achievable benefit; doubling cache size buys little; and adding tiers helps mainly by *collapsing misses* (shield effect), not by adding raw capacity. Tier content policy is a real choice: **inclusive** hierarchies duplicate upper-tier contents below (simpler coherence, unique capacity = largest tier), **exclusive** hierarchies hold each object at exactly one level (total capacity = sum of tiers, at the cost of migration traffic) ([Wikipedia: cache inclusion policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cache_inclusion_policy)).

## 7. Transferable patterns for Akashic Aurora

**7.1 The recall pipeline is a recursive resolver; give every stage its own cache and TTL discipline.** `context-name → Atlas/Track/Chapter delegation → Beat/skeleton → atom payload` is structurally `QNAME → NS chain → A record → object fetch`. DNS's lesson (Jung et al.) is that the *delegation* cache, not the leaf cache, carries the system: skeleton edges (which Chapters exist, which Beats a Chapter contains) change rarely and should be cached long and hot in L1; leaf bindings ("current atom for this Beat") are the volatile part; payloads are immutable and cache forever (§7.4). The 100–150ms budget is exactly a warm-delegation resolver profile: cold = several sequential lookups, warm = one. Instrument the pipeline per-stage the way resolver operators do — cached vs uncached latency histograms — so you know *which* layer misses are eating the budget.

**7.2 Negative caching is the highest-leverage import.** If only 4.5% of surfaced context helps, the correct answer to most recall queries is "nothing useful here" — and the internet proves absence is cacheable, TTL-able, even *provable*. Concretely: (a) cache `(canonicalized context signature) → NO_RELEVANT_LESSONS` as a first-class entry (RFC 2308's `<QNAME,QCLASS>` keying); (b) exploit the hierarchy like RFC 8020/8198 — because the ledger is append-only and serialized, the store can emit a signed range assertion "no atoms under `/atlas/trackX/chapterY` matching predicate P as of ledger seq N," which answers *never-before-seen* queries in that range instantly, NSEC-style; (c) invalidation of a negative entry is exact and cheap: any append with seq > N under that prefix voids it — a single-writer luxury DNS never had. The root servers spend ~55% of their capacity on NXDOMAIN ([APNIC](https://blog.apnic.net/2023/07/12/measuring-nxdomain-responses/)); a recall funnel where ~95% of surfaced material is noise should treat fast, cached, *confident* "no" as its primary product.

**7.3 Stale-while-revalidate recall: never block the agent.** RFC 5861's contract maps verbatim: on a recall hit whose ranking is past freshness, inject the cached bundle *now* and revalidate asynchronously; the next action gets the refreshed bundle. Add `stale-if-error`: if the ranker/embedding stage is down or over budget, degrade to the stale bundle rather than to silence. This converts the 100–150ms target from "the ranking must finish in 150ms" to "a cache lookup must finish in 150ms; ranking runs off the critical path" — the same latency-hiding move that lets CDNs survive slow origins.

**7.4 Supersession is already the versioned-URL pattern — commit to it and the coherence problem dissolves.** Atoms are never mutated, only superseded: that is RFC 8246 immutability plus cache-busting. Therefore: address payloads by immutable (ideally content-hash) IDs; mark them `immutable`-equivalent in every tier (L1/L2/L3 may hold them forever, no revalidation, no purge protocol); confine mutability to the tiny `name → current-atom` binding, resolved through one authority with short freshness or CAS. Fastly and Cloudflare each spent a decade of distributed-systems engineering to make global purge take 150ms ([Fastly](https://www.fastly.com/blog/over-a-decade-later-evolution-of-instant-purge), [Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/instant-purge/)); the versioned-name discipline makes that machinery unnecessary. The one place purge sneaks back in is negative entries (§7.2c) and "latest" bindings — keep that invalidation set small and event-hooked to the ledger append path.

**7.5 Popularity-tiered placement — but measure *utility*, not requests.** Zipf says a small head does most of the work and hit ratio grows only logarithmically with cache size ([Breslau](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~cao/papers/zipf-implications.html)); the funnel's 4.5% strongly suggests lesson *utility* is at least as skewed. So: log per-atom outcomes (surfaced → actually helped), pin the proven head into skeleton-adjacent L1, demote never-helpful atoms toward cold storage, and resist buying hit ratio with bigger caches — the log curve says placement and ranking beat capacity. Jung's finding that cache sharing saturates beyond 10–20 clients is a sizing warning: for a fleet of a few agents, one shared mid-tier plus per-agent L1 captures nearly all sharing benefit; deeper hierarchies are decoration.

**7.6 Interest-packet subscriptions: standing interests on name prefixes.** NDN's PIT holds unsatisfied Interests as routable state and aggregates duplicates. Import it as a subscription table over the Atlas namespace: an agent registers a standing interest in `/atlas/security/*` or a predicate; publication of a matching atom pushes it down every registered face, and N agents' identical standing queries collapse into one evaluation at publish time. This flips recall from poll-at-action-time to push-at-publish-time for "watch this area" workloads, and it is the natural transport for supersession notices: a superseding atom is pushed to exactly the parties holding interests in the superseded name's prefix.

**7.7 Self-certifying atoms for peer sharing; consistent hashing when (if) you shard.** Content-hash atom IDs make replicas self-authenticating, NDN-style — agents can hand each other recall bundles over the bus and verify them against the ID without trusting the sender ([CoNEXT 2009](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/co-next/2009/papers/Jacobson.pdf)). If the store ever shards across processes/machines, consistent hashing gives minimal-remap placement ([Karger](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/258533.258660)); Kademlia becomes relevant only when no node can hold the full index — far beyond present scale.

## 8. Where the analogy breaks

**Exact names vs fuzzy relevance.** Every mechanism above resolves an *exact* name to *exact* bytes. Recall must rank fuzzy relevance against a query that may never repeat verbatim. All the cache machinery therefore hangs off one prerequisite the internet never had to solve: canonicalizing a context into a stable name (quantized task signature). If canonicalization is weak, hit ratios collapse and every pattern in §7 degrades to decoration. Relatedly, CDN success metrics are the wrong objective: a CDN counts a hit as success; the funnel's 4.5% proves that *serving* cached content is not *helping*. Optimize precision-at-injection, a metric no CDN measures.

**TTLs assume time-shaped staleness; knowledge staleness is event-shaped.** A TTL is a bet that truth decays smoothly; supersession is a discrete event at an arbitrary moment. Immutability rescues payload caching completely (§7.4), but freshness of *bindings* and *negative entries* cannot honestly be TTL-managed — a cached "nothing here" is wrong the instant one atom lands. The fix (ledger-hooked invalidation) works only because appends serialize through one door; it is purge wearing a cheaper coat, and it stops being cheap the moment writes decentralize.

**Zipf is a crowd phenomenon.** α ≈ 0.7 emerges from millions of independent users; a two-agent fleet's query stream is bursty, task-correlated, and non-stationary — this week's project *is* the head of the distribution. Recency (LRU) may dominate popularity pinning; §7.5 must be validated against real traces before pinning anything.

**Hierarchical names partition authority, not relevance.** DNS delegation works because `example.com` strictly contains its subtree. Atlas→Track→Chapter is semantic; relevant atoms for one query can span prefixes, so longest-prefix match can silently amputate cross-cutting recall, and prefix-scoped negative assertions (§7.2b) are sound only for genuinely prefix-local predicates.

**Geography, adversaries, and decentralization are absent.** Anycast and geo-DNS fight the speed of light; NDN signatures fight untrusted routers; DHTs fight the absence of a central index. A single-host system with a trusted central store has none of these problems — Kademlia in particular is a solution shopping for a problem below ~dozens of nodes. And NDN's one-Interest-one-Data discipline means §7.6's "standing interest" is really pub/sub wearing NDN naming — a useful discipline, not a network-layer transplant.
