## 0. The one-page version

1. **A frame is a recipe, and a frame debugger is a logic analyser on the bus that carries it.** The CPU sends the GPU a
   list of commands: bind this shader, bind these textures, set this blend mode, draw these triangles into that target.
   A frame capture records that list with every argument and replays it, so you can stop after any command and look at
   every "register" (the pipeline state) and every "memory bank" (textures and render targets). It is a logic analyser,
   not an oscilloscope: replay does not reproduce real timing [read: RenderDoc FAQ]. Timing needs a separate tool (PIX
   timing captures, AMD's Radeon GPU Profiler).
2. **Frame debuggers are for our own builds, our own pages and open-source projects.** RenderDoc's author states the tool
   is for debugging your own programs and refuses to discuss captures of programs you did not create [read]. Online games
   with anti-cheat are never a target. For other people's games, the lawful and usually sufficient evidence is their
   *behaviour on screen* (our own recordings, public video) plus what their authors chose to publish (talks, breakdowns).
3. **A screenshot is display-referred; the look was made scene-referred.** By the time a frame reaches a video, light has
   been tone-mapped, clipped, gamma-encoded, quantised to 8 bits and usually chroma-subsampled and compressed. Every
   measurement must first undo what can be undone (the display curve) and name what cannot (anything above the clip).
   Measured today on a house frame: treating code values as light more than doubled a falloff estimate (126.5 km against
   a true 55 km); decoding with the right curve gave 57.5 km [ran, W2].
4. **Calibrate every ruler on a look we built before pointing it at one we did not.** Each worked example below measures
   a frame whose answer the house knows (a house shader card, a synthetic frame) and reports the ruler's error. Some
   rulers are excellent (a Hald LUT capture reproduced a grade within 1 code value; a cleaned phase correlation recovered
   screen shake within 0.09 px), and some are traps (a bloom threshold fitted from a screenshot came out 2.83 against a
   true 1.0) [ran, W3-W5].
5. **Decompose before you measure.** Real effects are layers: two textures scrolling at different speeds, a glow on a
   core, sparks over smoke, a shake over a hit-stop. Measuring the sum as one thing gives a confident wrong number
   (W2 motion; W5 shake: 44 px of error before the moving particles were removed, 0.09 px after) [ran].
6. **Read the authors before reading the pixels.** VFX culture is unusually generous: Blizzard, Riot, Sucker Punch and
   many artists have explained their own effects in talks and style guides. The author's own account outranks a third
   party's teardown, which outranks our measurement; our measurement then checks whether the shipped game matches.
7. **Mechanics and techniques are free to learn; assets and distinctive presentation are not.** Copyright never covers
   an idea, procedure or method (17 U.S.C. 102(b)); it does cover textures, models, shader source and, as *Tetris v.
   Xio* shows, a game's particular visual expression of its rules, down to effects such as its ghost piece and garbage
   lines. Rebuild from measured behaviour in our own art, never from extracted assets. (Not legal advice; section 13.)

## 1. Boundaries this report keeps

**Scope.** Understanding, learning from design, interoperability, analysing software we own or are permitted to analyse,
security research on our own systems, clean-room rebuilding. Not: cracking DRM or licence checks, piracy, cheating in
online games, anti-cheat or anti-tamper bypass, malware, intrusion. In graphics terms that means:

| In scope | Out of scope |
|---|---|
| Capturing frames of house pages (Shader Lab, piano, cockpit, First Light), house builds, open-source engines and sample projects we build ourselves | Injecting a capture layer into a commercial game, and above all into any online game with anti-cheat or any title with DRM or anti-tamper |
| Measuring public video and our own recordings of games we play (timings, palettes, motion, counts) | Ripping textures, meshes, flipbooks or shaders out of game files or GPU memory of games we do not own |
| Reading talks, style guides and breakdowns published by the effects' authors | Re-publishing other people's frames, textures or shader code in the public repo |
| Rebuilding a technique in our own code and art, and comparing it with the reference by measurement | Shipping a look-alike that copies a game's distinctive presentation (trade dress, look and feel) |

**Host safety.** This machine runs with Windows Defender off and updates blocked by the owner's choice; that posture is
not argued here. Consequences for this domain:

- **Untrusted inputs go in a disposable VM or sandbox, never on the host, never in the repo.** That covers capture files
  someone else made (`.rdc`, PIX `.wpix`, Spector JSON from a stranger), downloaded "effect packs", sample projects from
  unknown sources, and video files from untrusted sites (media decoders have had real parsing vulnerabilities [known]).
  `house-inventory.md` records that no disposable environment is enabled today (its gap G1).
- **Frame debuggers inject into the process they capture.** Only ever attach them to programs we built or trust.
- **GPU work is Daniel's call on this host.** The house has an AMD display-driver crash (TDR) history; every house render
  runs on SwiftShader, the CPU, under the GPU lock (`arsenal/GPU-LOCK.md`, `arsenal/shaders/headless.mjs`) [read]. A native
  frame debugger replays the frame on the real GPU, and a heavy replay can trip the same watchdog [inferred]. RenderDoc,

- **Never port 8793** (Daniel's server). Browser-based capture lanes use the lab server on its own port, as `headless.mjs`

## 3. Concepts, plainly

### 3.1 What a frame is

Think of the GPU as a factory floor with thousands of identical small workers and a foreman who reads work orders. The
CPU writes the work orders. One frame is a stack of them, typically hundreds to thousands. A public example: one frame of
GTA V on PC, as studied by Adrian Courrèges, used 4,155 draw calls, 1,113 textures and 88 render targets [read].

The vocabulary, each with its hardware analogy:

- **Draw call.** One work order: "run this program over these triangles". The triangles are usually two per sprite or
  one big one covering the screen (a *fullscreen pass*, which is how most post-processing and all Shadertoy-style shaders
  run). Analogy: one DMA transfer plus one kernel launch.
- **Shader.** The small program each worker runs. A *vertex shader* places corners; a *fragment* (pixel) *shader* decides
  each pixel's colour; a *compute shader* is general-purpose work (particle simulation, blur). Analogy: microcode.
- **Pipeline state.** Every switch the hardware reads while drawing: blend mode (does this pixel *add* to what is there,
  or *replace* it?), depth test (is something closer already drawn?), culling, viewport, which textures are bound to which
  slots. Analogy: the register file. Most "why does it look like that" answers live here, not in the shader.
- **Render target.** An off-screen picture a pass draws into, which a later pass reads. Bloom, shadows, the G-buffer of a
  deferred renderer and every "buffer" of a Shadertoy shader are render targets. Analogy: an intermediate buffer in a DSP
  chain. Its *format* matters: an 8-bit target clips at 1.0; a 16- or 32-bit float target keeps light brighter than white,
  which is what makes bloom possible.
- **Pass.** A group of draws into the same target for one purpose: shadow maps, the main scene, transparent effects, then
  post-processing (bloom, tone mapping, anti-aliasing), then UI. Courrèges' GTA V walk-through lists fifteen stages from
  environment cubemap to UI, with particles inside the transparent pass and bloom fused with tone mapping [read].
- **Draw order.** Opaque things are usually drawn front-to-back (so hidden pixels are skipped); transparent things,
  including almost all particles, back-to-front (so blending composes correctly). Particles are the classic *overdraw*
  problem: many large transparent quads stacked over the same pixels, each one paid for in full. On a phone this is the
  first thing that kills frame rate [known].

### 3.2 How a game effect is usually built

An effect such as an arrow impact is rarely one thing. Effect artists build it in layers [known; the layering habit and
the primary/secondary split are described in Love 2013 as reconstructed by Seavert, and in Riot's guide, both read]:

- **Core** (the bright centre, often additive), **glow** (a soft halo, sometimes a separate sprite, sometimes the bloom
  pass), **secondary elements** (sparks, debris, smoke), **distortion** (a heat-haze pass that bends the frame behind),
  **light** (a short-lived dynamic light), **decal** (a scorch left on the ground), **camera** (shake, hit-stop), **sound**.
- **Sprites and flipbooks.** A particle is usually a camera-facing quad textured from a *flipbook* (a sprite sheet of
  animation frames; Unreal calls it SubUV). Reading the frame rate of a flipbook (often 12-30 fps, independent of the game's
  frame rate) is a common measurement [known].
- **Greyscale textures plus a gradient map.** Diablo III's effects layered greyscale textures scrolling at randomised
  speeds, multiplied together, and coloured afterwards by a hand-painted gradient: motion without visible repetition, and
  colour that can be changed per skill without new textures (Julian Love, GDC 2013; reconstructed by Nick Seavert for
  JangaFX) [read]. The house aurora card uses the same trick: two noise rows drifting in opposite directions (W2).
- **Erosion / dissolve.** A noise texture compared against a threshold that rises over the particle's life, so the sprite
  eats itself away instead of fading uniformly [known].
- **Soft particles.** Fading a sprite where it nears solid geometry (by comparing depths), so flat quads do not show
  hard intersection lines [known].
- **Blend modes.** *Additive* (light: fire, magic, glows; order-independent, never darkens), *alpha* (smoke, dust;
  order-dependent), *premultiplied alpha* (one mode that can do both) [known].
- **Timing.** Riot's VFX style guide (Jin Yang, 2017) teaches an anticipation, a climax and a dissipation stage, with
  dissipation there to let the player's brain catch up; and it ranks gameplay readability above theme, with value,
  saturation and size tiers that match a spell's importance [read: VFX Apprentice's summary; the original Riot page now
  redirects]. For an Archero-style game, where dodging projectiles *is* the game, these readability rules are the
  most transferable knowledge in this report.

### 3.3 Particle systems and shader graphs, as a teardown target

A particle system is a small simulation with a fixed anatomy, the same in Unity's VFX Graph, Unreal's Niagara, Godot,
Effekseer and every hand-written one [known; Niagara's content examples are organised the same way, read]:

| Stage | Parameters a teardown tries to recover |
|---|---|
| **Spawn** | burst count, continuous rate, spawn shape (point, cone, ring, mesh surface) |
| **Initialise** | lifetime (and its random range), initial velocity, size, rotation, colour, flipbook start frame |
| **Update** | gravity and drag, curl-noise or vector-field forces, size over life, colour over life, alpha over life, collisions |
| **Render** | sprite, ribbon/trail or mesh; material; blend mode; sorting; flipbook rate; soft-particle fade; lit or unlit |

A shader graph (Unity Shader Graph, Unreal's material editor, Blender's node editor) is a schematic of a pixel shader:
boxes for texture samples, maths and outputs, wires for data. Reading one is reading a schematic. The house's own parts
catalogue (`py -m arsenal shader atlas`, families A to M) is a schematic vocabulary for the same job: every measured trait
of a look should map to a named part or to a gap in the atlas (section 10).

### 3.4 Scene-referred and display-referred: what a picture can and cannot contain

The house's colour report (`research/reviewed/shader-school-2026-10-13/color-post.md`, section 0) puts it as two
currencies: *light* (scene-linear; double the number, double the photons) and *code values* (display-encoded; bent by a
transfer curve so 8 bits land where the eye can tell differences). A captured frame is code values. Before any
measurement of brightness, falloff, blur radius or energy:

- **Decode** the transfer curve you believe was used (sRGB, a plain power, or the game's own). If you do not know it,
  measure it on something known in the frame, or report the measurement as display-referred.
- **Name the clip.** Anything that reached white is gone; brightness above the clip is unrecoverable from that pixel.
  Halos around a clipped core still carry information (W4).
- **Video adds more loss** [known]: limited range (16-235 instead of 0-255), BT.709 colour primaries and matrix, chroma
  subsampled to half resolution in each direction (4:2:0), and a codec that smears fine grain and small sparks first.
  `py agent_cli.py media probe` reports codec, frame rate and keyframe interval [read: media verb list]; check the colour
  range before trusting a black level.

## 4. The method, step by step

This is the playbook. It applies to an own-build frame capture and to a look read from footage alike.

1. **Route the target** (section 14a). Write down what the artifact is, where it came from, and the **permission
   basis**: ours, open source, licence allows study, or public footage watched for measurement only.
2. **Write the question checklist before looking.** Example for an impact effect: How long is each stage, in frames? How
   many elements, and which blend mode each? What palette, and how saturated against the scene? Does it bloom, and from
   what brightness? Is there a camera shake or a hit-stop, and how long? What does it cost (draws, overdraw)?
3. **Summary first.** For a capture: the pass list and draw counts, then the render-target list with formats. For
   footage: a contact sheet and a storyboard (`py agent_cli.py media grab <video> --at ... --sheet`, `media shots`,
   `py -m arsenal storyboard <video>`), then pick the few frames that matter. Do not open textures or step frames before
   the overview says where to look.
4. **Decompose into layers.** Passes in a capture; effect layers in footage (core, glow, sparks, smoke, distortion,
   camera). For each layer note which evidence isolates it (a separate draw call; a distinct colour; a distinct spatial
   scale; a distinct speed).
5. **Calibrate the ruler, then measure.** Before measuring the target, run the same script on a known look (a house card,
   a CPU twin frame, a synthetic) and record its error (W2-W5 show how). Measure in light, not code values.
6. **Inspect resources and configuration alongside code.** Blend state, texture contents, uniform values, render-target
   formats. Never infer behaviour from names.
7. **Corroborate with a different kind of evidence.** Static reading of a shader against its runtime capture; a
   measurement against the author's talk; and always, at the end, **rebuild and measure the rebuild with the same ruler**
   (an A/B under the same tool is the strongest evidence a look study can produce).
8. **Keep the ledger** (observation, inference, unknown, evidence id, confidence, search boundary) and **revisit the
   checklist**: each question answered, partially answered or unresolved.
9. **Rebuild clean-room.** The study writes a behaviour specification: numbers, timings, layer structure. The rebuild
   uses our own code (house modules) and our own art. Label every place where the rebuild makes a design choice that the
   evidence did not dictate.

## 6. Reading a frame

### 6.1 The walk, in any frame debugger

1. **Event list, collapsed.** Find the passes: markers if the app emits them, otherwise render-target switches. Count
   draws per pass. That is the frame's table of contents.
2. **Render-target list.** Name, size, format, how many times each is written and read. Float formats mean HDR; a chain of
   halving sizes means a blur or bloom pyramid; a target the size of the screen at quarter resolution means a cheap effect
   pass.
3. **The final image backwards.** Start at the last draw into the back buffer and walk upstream: which targets feed it,
   which passes feed those.
4. **One draw, fully.** For a chosen effect draw: the shader (source or translated), its bound textures (open them; read
   them, do not trust their names), uniforms, blend state, depth state, vertex count and instance count (instancing is how
   particle counts show up in a capture).
5. **Overlays.** Quad overdraw shows where particles stack; NaN/Inf/negative shows numerical bugs; clipping shows what
   hit black or white; pixel history shows every write to one pixel in order [read: RenderDoc texture viewer].
6. **Ledger.** Write the pass list as a finding with the capture's identity (file hash, build, frame number).

### 6.2 Worked example W1: predict the frame of a house page from its source (static; capture not run)

The house's Shadertoy-compatible runtime (`arsenal/web/shaderlab/toy-runtime.js`) states in its header, and implements in
`drawPass`, exactly what a frame does [read]:

- Buffers render first, in the order the shader was saved (not by letter), each into a float target (RGBA32F when the
  float extensions allow, else RGBA16F, else RGBA8 with a warning), ping-ponging between two textures so a self-read sees
  the previous frame. Then the cubemap pass (six draws, one per face), then Image. Every pass is **one draw of three
  vertices** (a single triangle covering the screen) [read: lines 1565-1605].
- The context is WebGL2 with `preserveDrawingBuffer: true`, so the canvas can be read back after the frame [read].

**Prediction for the atmos-aurora card** (only Common and Image passes): one `useProgram`, the uniform uploads, one
`bindFramebuffer(null)`, one `viewport`, one `drawArrays(TRIANGLES, 0, 3)` per frame. **Prediction for a shader with
Buffer A reading itself:** two draws per frame, the first into Buffer A's back target, the second into the canvas sampling
Buffer A's newest texture; a resize frame adds a framebuffer blit that keeps the overlapping pixels.

**Status:** [inferred from source]. A Spector.js capture of the page in the house's headless SwiftShader lane would turn
these into observations; that lane does not exist yet (gap G8 in `house-inventory.md`; section 14c). This is the REA
order: static evidence first, a prediction written down, then the runtime capture to confirm or refute it.

## 7. Reverse engineering a look from video or screenshots

Every technique below was tried on frames whose answer the house knows. Numbers are from the receipts.

### 7.1 Getting clean frames

- **Prefer extraction to stepping.** `media grab` pulls frames at exact times in one pass and can make a contact sheet;
  stepping backwards in a player is slow and fragile on long-GOP video [read: mpv manual].
- **Check the real frame rate.** A 60 fps recording of a 30 fps game holds every frame twice; a variable frame rate
  recording lies about time. `mpdecimate` or a frame-difference scan shows held frames [read: FFmpeg filters]; in W5 the
  difference scan found the planted hold exactly.
- **Check the range and matrix** before reading any black level (section 3.4).
- **Own recordings beat downloads.** Our own lossless or high-bitrate capture of play keeps grain and sparks that a
  streaming re-encode removes [known].

### 7.2 Worked example W2: read the house aurora card from pixels, then grade the reading against its source [ran]

**Target.** `state/arsenal/shaders/cards/atmos-aurora/t0.00.png` and `t2.00.png`: the house test card of the aurora
module, 480x270, rendered on SwiftShader. The source is ours and known (`arsenal/web/glsl/cards/atmos-aurora.card`):
a green-to-red curtain whose emission rises over 6 km above a wavering lower edge (the *hem*) and decays with a 55 km
scale height, a 557.7 nm green of (0.222, 1, 0), a "hue-safe" display gamma of 2.2 applied to the colour's magnitude only,
and rays made of two noise rows drifting in opposite directions. The measurements below used only the right-hand panel's
pixels; the source was used afterwards, as the grading key. Receipt: `graphics-vfx-receipts/aurora_lookread.{py,json}`.

**Step 0, the stand-in.** The house's CPU twins (`arsenal/shaders/twins/atmos.py`, `tone.py`) recompute the panel in
Python. Against the GPU-path PNGs the twin is exact to the code value: mean absolute difference 0.0 codes, worst pixel 1
code, at both t = 0 and t = 2. That made it safe to generate extra frames the renderer never drew (used in step 5).

**1. Palette (OKLab k-means, k = 5, area-weighted).** Night sky #150a1d (31.3% of the panel, OKLab L 0.174); dim red crown
#310e14 (27.4%); green body #1d7409 (20.4%); a darker green #24470e (15.3%); bright green rays #26a307 (5.6%, L 0.625).
Observed. Clustering in OKLab rather than RGB groups colours the way the eye does (Ottosson, 2020) [read].

**2. A constant from pixels.** Among the 251 brightest green pixels, the median red-to-green ratio is 0.2238; the source
constant is 0.222. Observation matched the source to under 1%, and it confirms an inference about the display curve: a
per-channel gamma would have changed that ratio, and this one did not, so the gamma acts on magnitude, not channels.

**3. A falloff from pixels, three ways.** Fitting the brightness decay with height in the pure-green band (128-160 km):

| Decode assumed before fitting | Scale height measured | Error |
|---|---|---|
| none (code values treated as light) | 126.5 km | +130% |
| the standard sRGB curve | 59.3 km | +8% |
| magnitude gamma 2.2 (the true display transform) | 57.5 km | +4.5% |

A lower band (118-158 km) gave 131.3, 60.6 and 59.7 km: the residual bias grows where the 6 km rise term still matters.
**Lesson: decode first; the wrong currency doubles the answer.**

**4. An edge from pixels.** The hem, found per column as the half-maximum on the rising side and offset by the profile's
known half-height (2.76 km above the hem), tracks the true hem with correlation 0.991 and 1.71 km RMS error, about 1.8
pixel rows at 0.963 km per row. Observation: the edge; inference: the offset (a blind reader would have to fit it).

**5. Motion of two scrolling layers.** Truth from the source: the coarse ray layer drifts at +5.705 px/s, the fine one
at -4.645 px/s (opposite directions, the dual-scroll trick of section 3.2). Readings:

| Method | Coarse layer (px/s) | Fine layer (px/s) |
|---|---|---|
| Ruler check: the coarse noise row alone, from the twin | +5.88 to +6.00 | n/a |
| The two shipped frames, 2 s apart, column signal split by a low-pass at 6 px | +7.5 | -4.5 |
| Twin frames at 1/30 s to 1 s apart, hem-independent column peak, band-split | 0.0 to +4.88 (depends on band and spacing) | -3.25 to -4.25 |

What this says. **Direction** of both layers was read correctly once the spacing was long enough, and the fine layer's
speed came within 3-30%. The coarse layer's speed was badly biased, because the rays are a *threshold of the sum* of the
two layers (a smoothstep), so the visible pattern does not translate rigidly at either layer's speed, and at short
spacings the fine layer dominates every band. The first attempt also read a coarse speed of zero because the hem's own
travelling waves were moving the column brightness; switching to each column's peak (where the hem's position does not
matter) removed that, a decomposition step. **Lesson:** from pixels, quote layer directions and rough speeds, and confirm
by rebuilding and comparing side by side; do not trust a single correlation peak.

### 7.3 Colour grading: two cases, very different power

**Case A, we can run the pipeline** (our own shader or app, or a tool we are licensed to use, applied to an image of
ours). Feed it an **identity Hald CLUT**: an image whose pixels enumerate a cube of colours in a known order. Whatever comes
back *is* the colour transform, read out as a 3D lookup table. FFmpeg makes one (`haldclutsrc`) and applies one
(`haldclut`; `lut3d` reads `.cube` files) [read].

**Worked example W3 [ran]** (`synthetic_rulers.py`, part B). A house-written "mystery grade" (white balance, exposure, a
filmic shoulder, teal shadows and warm highlights, saturation, an S-curve) applied to identity Hald images, the recovered
LUT then applied to two house images (the aurora card, the geodesic avatar snapshot) and to an image holding every colour
of a 64-level cube, with the grade applied directly as the truth:

| Hald level | Colours in the cube | Mean error | 99th percentile | Worst |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 (64x64 image) | 16^3 = 4,096 | 0.48 codes | 4 | 5 |
| 6 (216x216) | 36^3 = 46,656 | 0.19 | 2 | 3 |
| 8 (512x512) | 64^3 = 262,144 | 0.04 | 1 | 1 |

**When a LUT cannot capture the look.** A LUT is a per-pixel colour function. Two tests catch anything else: place the
Hald image at two positions (a vignette or any position-dependent effect changes the answer), and render it with 1-pixel
and 4-pixel cells (any blur, bloom or sharpening mixes neighbours). Results, worst difference in code values:

| Pipeline | Position test | Neighbour test |
|---|---|---|
| pure grade | 0 | 0 |
| grade + vignette | 85 | 39 |
| grade + 1 px blur | 77 | 101 |

Both zero means the LUT *is* the look; anything else means the look has spatial parts that must be separated first.

**Case B, only the graded footage exists.** The operator itself is not identifiable from one graded image, only its
fingerprints. Measure, in OKLab or linear light: black and white points (are blacks lifted, highlights rolled off?), the
tint of shadows, mid-tones and highlights separately (split toning), saturation as a function of lightness, hue shifts of
known memory colours (skin, sky, foliage), and the palette (W2's method). Then rebuild with a small, explicit grade (lift,
gamma, gain or ASC CDL; see `color-post.md` section 9) and compare statistics. Statistical colour transfer is the
automated version: Reinhard, Ashikhmin, Gooch and Shirley (2001) match the mean and spread per channel in a decorrelated
colour space; Pitié, Kokaram and Dahyot (2007) transfer the whole colour distribution [read]. Both move *our* image toward
the reference's statistics; neither tells you what grade the reference used.

### 7.4 Bloom: what a screenshot can tell you

**Worked example W4 [ran]** (`synthetic_rulers.py`, part C). A frame with five tiny emitters of known brightness (0.6,
1.5, 4, 16 and 64 times display white), a thresholded bloom built as a sum of six Gaussian blurs at doubling radii (1.5
to 48 px, threshold 1.0, strength 0.04; a simplified member of the mip-chain bloom family, of which Jorge Jimenez's Call of
Duty: Advanced Warfare pipeline at SIGGRAPH 2014 is the reference [read]; see `color-post.md` section 6.5), then clamped
and sRGB-encoded to 8 bits, like an LDR game frame.

- **Cores clip.** The emitters read 203, 255, 255, 255, 255 code values: four of five are indistinguishable at the core.
- **Halos remember.** Halo energy (radii 6-60 px, display-linear) was 0, 0, 0.0035, 0.0273 and 0.1323. The 1.5 emitter
  blooms, but its halo is under one code value and vanishes in 8 bits.
- **The threshold trap.** A straight line through the halo energies of the three clearly blooming emitters puts the
  threshold at **2.83**; the truth is 1.0. The same fit on the float render target (what a frame debugger shows for our
  own build, before clamping) gives **0.992**.
- **The tail.** Distance at which the halo falls to 50% and to 10% of its value at 4 px: mip-chain bloom 6 and 13 px
  (ratio 2.17); a single Gaussian 9 and 14 px (ratio 1.56). The long tail is the mip chain's signature. Measured on raw
  code values instead of light, the same bloom reads 8 and 20 px: the encoding curve lifts faint tails.

What can honestly be said from a screenshot: whether bloom exists; whether unclipped emitters bloom (here the 203-code
one did not, so the threshold is at or above display white, an inference); the tail shape (single blur versus chain);
radii as a fraction of frame height. What cannot: the threshold value or the strength, separately from the tone mapper.
For our own build, read the bloom render target in a frame debugger or render a **bloom test card** with a ramp of known
emitters (the house card system is the natural home; section 14c).

### 7.5 Particles, hit-stop and screen shake

**Worked example W5 [ran]** (`synthetic_rulers.py`, part D). Synthetic 320x180 frames: soft additive particles over a
static textured world.

**Counting.** Two simple counters bracket the truth from opposite sides as density rises:

| True count | Connected components (threshold, 8-connected) | Local maxima |
|---|---|---|
| 25 | 23 | 26 |
| 100 | 84 | 99 |
| 400 | 306 | 478 |

Connected components merge overlapping particles (undercount); local maxima split merged blobs into several peaks
(overcount). Report the bracket and the trend, never one number; above a few hundred on screen, report density.

**Hit-stop** (the game holds the frame for a few frames on impact; Jan Willem Nijman's *The Art of Screenshake*, INDIGO
2013, and Jonasson and Purho's *Juice It or Lose It*, GDC Europe 2012, are the canonical talks on this kind of feel
[read]). A four-frame hold planted at frames 30-33:

| Clip | Zero-difference frames | Mean difference, held vs other frames | Pixels changed by more than 8 codes after a 1.5 px blur, held vs other |
|---|---|---|---|
| lossless, no grain | exactly 30-33 | 0.0 vs 2.67 codes | 0% vs 6.45% |
| animated grain + JPEG quality 50 | none | about 2.0 vs 4.78 codes | 0% vs 6.52% |

Real games often keep grain, UI and some effects animating during a hold, and compression adds noise, so "identical
frames" finds nothing. Blur away fine noise and count *structural* change instead; mask UI where it keeps moving.

**Screen shake.** Ten frames of decaying shake (true offsets from 4 px down to 0). Phase correlation of each frame
against a pre-shake reference gave an RMS error of **43.97 px** on raw frames (the moving particles dominate the
correlation) and **0.09 px** after a grey-level opening removed the small moving foreground first. Decompose first: the
camera is read from the static world, and only once the moving things are taken out.

### 7.6 Motion, timing and flipbooks (method notes)

- **Timing in frames, not seconds,** at the game's true frame rate (7.1). Stage lengths (anticipation, climax,
  dissipation) are the most transferable numbers in an effect [inferred from Riot's guide].
- **Easing:** track one feature's position per frame (template matching or optical flow), plot it, and fit the curve
  family (linear, exponential approach, spring). Report the fit's residual.
- **Flipbook rate:** within one particle, count frames until the sprite's shape changes; a flipbook running at 15 fps in a
  60 fps game changes every fourth frame.
- **Sampling rule (W2):** an aperiodic pattern moving more than about half its feature size between two samples aliases
  like a wagon wheel; sample densely, or measure the slow layer with long spacings and the fast one with short ones.

## 12. Mistakes that waste time

1. **Measuring code values as if they were light.** W2: 126.5 km instead of 55 km.
2. **Trusting a ruler that was never calibrated.** Every script in this report has a case where its first version was
   wrong (the zero coarse speed in W2, the 44 px shake error in W5) and the calibration target showed it.
3. **Fitting hidden parameters from clipped frames.** W4: a threshold of 2.83 against 1.0.
4. **Reading a technique off a name.** Texture, function and pass names are hints; blend state, texture content and maths
   are evidence.
5. **Quoting replay timings.** Frame-debugger replay is not real timing [read]; use a timing capture or a profiler.
6. **Reading ANGLE's translated shaders as if they were the page's GLSL** when a native debugger captures a browser
   [read]; capture at the WebGL layer for the page's own code.
7. **Capturing everything at once.** Start with one frame and the pass list; on SwiftShader a heavy capture can blow the
   house's three-minute burst budget [inferred from `GPU-LOCK.md`].
8. **Stepping backwards through compressed video.** Extract frames instead [read].
9. **Assuming the recording's frame rate is the game's.** Check for held frames first.
10. **Measuring grain, sparks or fine noise from a streaming re-encode.** The codec removes them first [known].
11. **One number for a particle count.** Report the bracket (W5).
12. **Calling a LUT "the look"** without the position and neighbour tests (W3).
13. **Matching by eye across colour-managed and unmanaged viewers.** Compare numbers, not impressions.
14. **Repeating an identical analysis** hoping for a different answer; change the input or the question first (REA).
15. **Taking assets "just to study them".** It is the step that turns a lawful study into infringement and, for protected
    titles, possibly circumvention (section 13); it also breaks the house licence line.
16. **Live GPU frame debugging on this host without Daniel's ruling** (TDR history; section 1).

## 13. Legal and safety notes (not legal advice)

This is orientation for planning, not legal advice; laws differ by country and change. When a real target is chosen,
the permission basis is decided before work starts, and anything unclear goes to Daniel (and, if it matters, to a
lawyer). `house-inventory.md` section 1 carries the general reverse-engineering note; this section adds what is specific
to graphics.

**United States.**

- *Ideas versus expression.* Copyright does not extend to "any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation"
  (17 U.S.C. 102(b)) [read]. Rendering techniques, effect structures and game mechanics are ideas; shader source code,
  textures, models, flipbooks, sounds and screen layouts are expression. Techniques may still be patented, which copyright
  analysis does not clear [known].
- *Look and feel.* In *Tetris Holding v. Xio Interactive*, 863 F. Supp. 2d 394 (D.N.J. 2012), the court held the game's
  rules unprotectable but found that a clone copied protected expression: the pieces' design and colours, their movement
  and rotation, the playing field, and visual effects such as garbage lines and the ghost piece; it also found trade dress
  infringement, calling the colours and styles "arbitrary flourishes unrelated to how the game worked" [read: Loeb & Loeb
  summary quoting the opinion]. For effects this matters directly: an effect's *function* (show where the projectile will
  land) is free; a particular game's way of drawing it can be protected.
- *Anti-circumvention.* 17 U.S.C. 1201(a)(1) forbids circumventing a technological measure that controls access to a
  work; 1201(f) permits circumvention by a lawful user "for the sole purpose of identifying and analyzing" elements needed
  for interoperability of an independent program, if not previously readily available; 1201(j) covers security testing
  with the owner's authorisation [read]. The 2024 rulemaking's exemptions (37 C.F.R. 201.40) include good-faith security
  research on lawfully acquired devices and video-game preservation in narrow cases [read]. None of these covers lifting
  assets out of a protected game for a look study. Attaching a capture layer to a game protected by DRM or anti-tamper may
  itself be circumvention [inferred]; it is out of scope here regardless.
- *Fair use of code for interoperability.* *Sega v. Accolade*, 977 F.2d 1510 (9th Cir. 1992): disassembly was fair use
  where it was "the only way to gain access to the ideas and functional elements" and there was a legitimate reason
  [read]. *Sony v. Connectix*, 203 F.3d 596 (9th Cir. 2000): intermediate copying of the PlayStation BIOS while building a
  non-infringing emulator was fair use [read]. Both concern access to functional elements for compatibility, not taking
  art; they do not license copying a look.
- *Contracts.* EULAs and terms of service often forbid reverse engineering and attaching tools; US courts have enforced
  such terms (cases in `house-inventory.md` section 1). Online games' terms and anti-cheat systems treat injected capture
  layers as cheating; that is a hard no here.

**European Union.**

- *Observe, study, test.* Directive 2009/24/EC art. 5(3) lets a person entitled to use a program "observe, study or test
  the functioning of the program" to find its ideas and principles while loading, displaying, running it [read]. Watching
  and measuring what a program puts on screen during ordinary use is the core of that permission [inferred]. Article 8
  makes contract terms contrary to art. 5(3) or art. 6 null and void [read]. The CJEU in *SAS Institute v World
  Programming* (C-406/10, 2012) held that a program's functionality, its programming language and its data file formats are
  not protected expression, and that the art. 5(3) right cannot be contracted away [read: case summaries].
- *Decompilation* (art. 6) is allowed only for interoperability, by a licensee, limited to the necessary parts, and the
  information may not be used to make a program substantially similar in expression [read]. A look study needs none of it.

**Community licences and policies.**

- *RenderDoc's policy:* the tool is intended for debugging your own programs; captures of commercial games you did not
  create, or of services such as Google Maps, are not discussed in its official channels; engine-based projects you made
  and open-source projects are fine [read: README]. The house adopts that line for every native frame debugger.
- *Shadertoy:* shaders without a stated licence fall under Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike 3.0
  [read: Shadertoy terms, via search]. That is why house modules are written fresh and corpus pictures stay in `state/`.
- *Sample projects* carry their own licences (Unity samples include third-party asset licences; Epic's under the Unreal
  Engine EULA). Learn from them freely; check before reusing any asset.
- *Footage of other people's games:* fine to watch and measure privately; keep their frames out of the public repo (store
  under `state/`), publish only numbers and our own rebuilds. Publishing excerpts is a separate fair-use or quotation
  question [inferred].

**Host safety recap.** Untrusted captures, effect packs, sample projects from unknown sources and downloaded video are
opened only inside a disposable VM or sandbox (none is enabled yet: inventory gap G1), never executed on the host, never
committed. Native GPU replay is Daniel's call (TDR history).

| One of our shaders: "what will it cost on RDNA?" | Radeon GPU Analyzer (offline) | Machine code, register pressure, static instruction counts | Runtime frequency of branches; real frame time |

| A commercial game's files or process | **Stop.** Route to its behaviour on screen and its authors' published material | (see the rows above) | Not a frame-debugger or extraction target in this house |

### 14b. Lessons the house should keep

1. **Use when** measuring any falloff, glow radius or gradient from a screenshot or video, **before** fitting: decode the
   display transform to light first, and say which transform you assumed. **Don't when** the transform is unknown; measure
   it on something known in the frame first (W2: 126.5 km with no decode against a true 55 km).
2. **Use when** a new measuring script is written, **before** pointing it at a target: run it on a look the house built
   with known parameters (a card, a CPU twin frame, a synthetic) and record its error beside every later result. **Don't
   when** tempted to skip it because the target "looks simple"; every ruler in W2-W5 failed once before calibration
   caught it.
3. **Use when** a colour look comes from a pipeline we can run, **before** hand-matching curves: push a level-8 identity
   Hald CLUT through it, read back the LUT, and run the position and neighbour tests. **Don't when** either test is
   non-zero; the look has spatial parts (vignette, bloom, blur, grain) to separate first.
4. **Use when** estimating a bloom threshold or strength, **before** fitting on screenshots: read the float bloom target in
   a frame capture of our own build, or render a test card of known emitters. **Don't when** only 8-bit frames exist;
   report "halo only on clipped emitters, threshold at or above white" instead of a number (W4: 2.83 against 1.0).
5. **Use when** estimating camera shake from footage, **before** phase correlation: remove the moving foreground (a
   grey-level opening or a median) and correlate against a pre-shake reference. **Don't when** no static world is in
   view; then it cannot be separated from object motion (W5: 43.97 px error raw, 0.09 px cleaned).
6. **Use when** reporting a particle count from frames, **before** writing a number: give the bracket from connected
   components (low) and local maxima (high). **Don't when** additive particles overlap heavily; report density.
7. **Use when** detecting hit-stop or held frames in real footage, **before** trusting zero-difference tests: blur away
   grain, count pixels that changed by more than about 8 codes, and mask animated UI. **Don't when** the footage is
   lossless and grain-free; then exact equality is enough.
8. **Use when** someone proposes attaching RenderDoc, PIX or any capture layer, **before** launching it: confirm the
   program is our build, an open-source project or explicitly permitted, and that Daniel has ruled on GPU use. **Don't
   when** the target is any online game, anything with DRM or anti-tamper, or the aim is to take assets.
9. **Use when** reading shader code from a native capture of a browser, **before** treating it as the page's code: it is
   ANGLE's translation (HLSL or SPIR-V). **Don't when** the question is about the page's own GLSL; capture at the WebGL
   layer with Spector.js.
10. **Use when** quoting how long a GPU frame or pass takes, **before** using a frame debugger's replay numbers: take a
    timing capture or profiler trace. **Don't when** only relative ordering of passes is needed.
11. **Use when** measuring the speed of layered scrolling textures, **before** quoting a speed: split by spatial scale,
    remove travelling envelopes, sample faster than half a feature per step, and check the ruler on one known layer.
    **Don't when** the layers are thresholded together and only a direction is needed; quote the direction (W2).
12. **Use when** a VFX breakdown by the effect's author exists, **before** measuring footage: read or watch it first and
    use the measurement to check it. **Don't when** the source is a third-party teardown presented as fact; treat it as a
    hypothesis.
13. **Use when** matching a reference look for a house game, **before** copying colours or shapes: take numbers (timings,
    contrast tiers, ranges), not assets or the distinctive arrangement of another game's screens. **Don't when** the
    reference is our own work or explicitly licensed for reuse.
