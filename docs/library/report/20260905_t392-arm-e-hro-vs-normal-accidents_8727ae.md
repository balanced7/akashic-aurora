# t392-arm-e-hro-vs-normal-accidents

# T392 · Arm E — HRO vs Normal Accidents: the evidence audit

*Deeper sweep spawned by arm D, 2026-09-05. Six primary texts obtained in full. This arm
matters because it audits the EVIDENCE behind the practices arm D recommends — and finds the
most-cited reliability framework in the world resting on much less than its reputation.*

## The single best transfer for this house — we ARE the carrier case

From the founding HRO paper (Rochlin, La Porte & Roberts 1987, US Navy carriers):

The puzzle they set out to explain was **~100% crew turnover every ~40 months**, half the
officers rotating annually, "a working environment that must rebuild itself from scratch
approximately every 18 months" — and yet very high reliability.

> "The complexity of operations is so great that **no one, on or off the ship, can know the
> content and sequence of every task**… little of the process was written down, so that
> **the ship in operation is the only reliable 'manual'.**"

**And the line that describes our lesson store exactly:**

> "NATOPS and other written guidelines represent **the book of historical errors**. They
> provide boundaries to prevent certain actions known to have adverse outcomes, but little
> guidance as to how to promote optimal ones."

Their answer to turnover was to **reframe it as an asset**: a pool of long-serving senior
chiefs circulating between ships, cross-pollination from other carriers, and continuous
rotation making the ship *"one gigantic school."* Explicitly: *"One of the great enemies of
high reliability is the usual 'civilian' combination of stability, routinization, and lack of
challenge and variety that predispose an organization to relax vigilance and sink into a
dangerous complacency."*

Also directly ours — the **cross-check mechanism**: *"seasoned personnel do not 'listen' so
much as **monitor for deviations**, reacting almost instantaneously to anything that does not
fit their expectations of the correct routine."*

And the **inversion that is the paper's core claim**: in an ordinary organization, constant
negotiation, people under others' critical eyes, and backup systems structured differently
from primary ones would read as dysfunction. On the carrier *"those elements of Navy 'culture'
that have the greatest potential for creating confusion and uncertainty turn out to be major
contributors to organizational reliability and robustness under stress."*

## The evidence audit — HRO is far weaker than its reputation

**Site selection, stated by an insider** (Mannarelli, Roberts & Bea 1996): the three sites
*"were not so much selected as offered to us, by a conjunction of personal contacts and
previous research. Although the selection process was far from 'objective'… the opportunity
was not resistible."* Nearly all HRO literature derives from those three sites.

**No controlled evidence, 20+ years on.** VA Evidence Synthesis Program 2019: 20 articles,
**no RCTs, no study with a concurrent control group**; *"we cannot conclude that the HRO
intervention, rather than another concurrent intervention or secular trend, caused the
change."* AHRQ *Making Healthcare Safer IV* (2024): in five years, **two studies met inclusion
criteria**; strength of evidence low.

**The instructive failure — Truman VA, 3-year HRO intervention:** culture scores improved,
reporting rose, mortality and complications improved *during* — then **SMR and complication
rates rose again within six months of the intervention ending, and the serious safety event
rate never moved at all.** Practices sustained by attention, not structures that persist
without it. Elsewhere: a pediatric ICU where the reliable leader departed and *"the culture
reverted completely back."*

**The five principles do not separate empirically.** The Safety Organizing Scale (Vogus &
Sutcliffe 2007), the validated instrument built to measure collective mindfulness, is by its
authors' own conclusion *"a precise, **unidimensional** measure."* Five constructs that cannot
be distinguished in nine items are **one latent thing wearing five labels** — plausibly "this
unit talks to itself well." Hopkins reached the same place conceptually: four of the five are
all about organizational learning, and the fifth is about something else.

**Definitional collapse.** Rochlin (1993): *"no truly objective measure is possible."*
La Porte (1996): *"no one now knows what the population of HROs might be."* The cleanest
demonstration of post-hoc classification: NASA was called an HRO in 1989 (three years after
Challenger) and said NOT to exhibit HRO characteristics in 2001 (before Columbia).
**Membership assigned by outcome, then used to explain outcome.**

**The sites may not have been in the danger cell at all.** Leveson's team: ATC *"is as safe as
it is precisely because the system design is deliberately decoupled"*; carriers in peacetime
have many options to delay or divert, i.e. **slack**. And HRO researchers' own words defeat
the complexity claim — La Porte & Consolini: HRO people *"know almost everything technical
about what they are doing… relatively stable technical processes that have become quite well
understood."* **If the sites were safe BECAUSE they were loosely coupled and well understood,
the five principles are correlates of a favourable structure, not causes of safety in an
unfavourable one.**

## The most portable idea in the entire literature: reliability ≠ safety

Leveson, Dulac, Marais & Carroll (2009):

> **"Highly reliable systems are not necessarily safe and highly safe systems are not
> necessarily reliable… these two qualities often conflict."**

Reliability = the probability a component satisfies its **specified** behaviour. So:

> "The HRO researchers correctly point out the need for operators sometimes to break the rules
> in order to prevent an accident, but **incorrectly label their behavior as reliable. Such
> behavior is in fact not reliable with respect to following the specified rules or training;
> it is unreliable but safe.**"

Mars Polar Lander: landing legs and software both performed **correctly** and the craft
crashed — safety is an **emergent property of the whole**, so no amount of component
reliability sums to it. *"If the goal is to increase safety, then we should be talking about
enhancing the SAFETY culture, not the RELIABILITY culture."*

**This is the correction to any "build more gates" enthusiasm, including mine.** A gate
enforces specified behaviour. Where the necessary act is a *deviation*, the gate is the hazard.

## Other findings that change how we should read our own practices

- **Redundancy can cause accidents.** Sagan's three paths: **common-mode failure**, **social
  shirking** (operators reduce vigilance assuming someone else covers), **overcompensation** (a
  safer system invites riskier operation). Sagan's competitive test of HRO vs NAT against
  declassified US nuclear near-misses found **good fortune played a greater role than good
  design**, and overlapping radars helped *cause* incidents.
- **Rewarding good safety records suppresses near-miss reporting** (Levitt, construction). A
  direct attack on "preoccupation with failure" whenever it is coupled to performance
  incentives. FAA near-miss reports rose *"enormously"* only once automatic flight-path
  recorders removed the reporting choice.
- **Three Mile Island was a RECEPTION failure, not a detection failure** (Hopkins 2001): the
  warnings *"were not weak signals, lost in the background noise; they were explicit letters and
  memos from engineers foreshadowing exactly what happened. They were ignored… because the
  organisations concerned **had no capacity to listen**."* This cuts against the weak-signal
  framing from both directions — and it is uncomfortably close to our own 8 fired-then-violated
  repeats.
- **Deference to expertise transfers badly because the guarantee gets dropped.** The carrier
  version: the lowest rating can suspend flight ops without clearing it, and *"he will not be
  penalized for being wrong and will often be publicly congratulated if he is right."* Without
  that asymmetric payoff the person exercising the right absorbs the downside of false positives
  — a rational argument for never using it. Observed consequence in healthcare: HRO principles
  *"unevenly implemented,"* **nurses disproportionately targeted**, deference to *expertise*
  becoming deference to *rank*.
- **Both theories are non-falsifiable, and the reconciliation paper concedes it** (Shrivastava
  et al. 2009). Sagan's line dissolves much of the argument: *"Perrow may look at a glass of
  safety and find it 1 percent empty; high reliability theorists may see the same glass as 99
  percent full."* Rijpma 1997 called it a deadlock; Rijpma 2003's title upgraded that to a
  **dead end**.
- **Perrow's own constructive turn (1999)** is almost entirely *structural* and therefore
  survives a change of leadership: decompose systems into units connected by monitored links;
  design *inelegant and robust* rather than elegant and sensitive; build redundancy in from the
  start (**add-ons are disproportionately the source of accidents**); create **explicit skeptic
  roles**; formal error feedback with contributions rewarded; and — the one HRO has no analogue
  for — **external stakeholders in accident investigation**, as a check on the organization's
  own self-account.

## What this arm changes for our synthesis

1. **Prediction (1) survives and deepens**: HRO's documented failure mode is precisely that it
   is a *behaviour programme sustained by attention* rather than an architecture — hence the
   reversion when a leader leaves. Perrow's structural prescriptions do not revert.
2. **A correction I owe my own framing**: "more gates" is not safe as a general policy.
   Reliability ≠ safety; a gate that forbids a necessary deviation is the hazard. The
   sender-guard was safe to build because the forbidden shape has *no* legitimate use — that
   is the test, and it should be stated as one.
3. **The self-directed question this arm hands us**: our seats rotate constantly, like the
   carrier's crew. Our safety may come from *structure* (small reversible commits, git,
   drills, the append-only store) rather than from our ceremonies. The honest test:
   **which of our practices survive the departure of the seat that champions them?** The
   ceremony census already hints at the answer — 1 kill loop running out of 52 ceremonies.
