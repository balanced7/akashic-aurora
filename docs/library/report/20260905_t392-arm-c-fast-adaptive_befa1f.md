# t392-arm-c-fast-adaptive

# T392 · Arm C — fast expert judgement in novel/adversarial situations

*Web research arm, 2026-09-05. This arm directly tested the registered forecast's `dies_when`
by hunting for a real-time deliberative interrupt that improves expert judgement. It found a
graveyard instead. Verification caveats preserved at the end.*

## The one-line answer

**You cannot insert a deliberative step into an expert's decision moment, because the expert
has no internal signal to trigger on.** Kahneman & Klein: people cannot know where their
intuitions came from, and there is **no subjective marker** distinguishing a correct intuition
from one produced by a bad heuristic. Every failed intervention below asks the expert to notice
something unnoticeable. Every successful one either shapes **orientation upstream**, fires on an
**external observable trigger**, or supplies **feedback downstream**.

## The primary data on how experts actually decide

Klein, Calderwood & Clinton-Cirocco: 26 fireground commanders, mean 23 years' experience,
**156 decision points — option comparison in fewer than 12%.** They generated ONE option,
evaluated it by mental simulation, modified or moved on. Serial, never parallel.

Recognition yields four by-products: **cues, expectancies, plausible goals, typical actions.**

> **Expectancies are the intervention point** — the only self-correcting element already inside
> the fast loop. The expert is *already* asking "if my read is right, what happens next?" You
> cannot add a comparison step, but you can install expectancies specific enough to be violated
> loudly, because checking them costs nothing extra.

Also: **validity ≠ certainty.** "Poker and warfare are examples" of environments both
high-validity and deeply uncertain — **adversarial does not mean unlearnable.** What an
adversary does is attack the specific cue→outcome links you rely on, converting a valid
environment into a *wicked* one where "the regularities that can be observed are misleading."

## THE GRAVEYARD — interventions that assumed deliberation

**Cognitive forcing strategies** (Sherbino/Norman 2014, n=191, EM residents) — all null:

| Outcome | CFS | Control | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initiated search for 2nd diagnosis | 52% | 48% | 0.91 |
| Correctly identified 2nd diagnosis | 54% | 48% | 0.13 |
| Uncommon correct dx (availability cases) | 45.2% | 44.6% | 0.98 |

Their own reading: a **ceiling on analytic processing** — further circumspection overwhelms
working memory without effect.

**Bias-awareness training** (Norman et al. 2017): of three studies testing whether teaching
bias-recognition reduces *errors*, **all showed no reduction**. General admonitions to slow
down, reflect, or be systematic "likely have minimal effect beyond slowing the process."

**The most instructive contrast in the whole round:** a **generic debiasing checklist** ("Did I
consider the inherent flaws of heuristic thinking?") produced **no** benefit; a **case-specific
checklist of 25 possible differential diagnoses** produced significant improvement.
**Content beat process.** The one that worked fed the pattern-matcher; the one that failed asked
the reasoner to audit itself.

**Why awareness training aims at the wrong target** — the bias blind spot (Pronin/Lin/Ross):
participants showing the better-than-average bias **insisted their self-assessments were
accurate even after reading a description of that very bias.**

**And the metacognitive signal exists but does not produce revision** (Monteiro 2015):
residents processed erroneous cases more slowly and were *more likely to select them for
review* — **and only 8% of errors resulted in a changed diagnosis.**

## The strongest counter-evidence, examined — it supports the thesis

Buçinca et al. 2021, three "cognitive forcing functions" (On-Demand, Update, Wait-30s), n=199.
They **significantly reduced overreliance**: 27% correct on wrong AI suggestions vs 8% with
plain explanations. But **none of the three asked anyone to deliberate** — they changed *when
information arrived*. Structural, not metacognitive. Three blunting findings: both conditions
still underperformed the **no-AI baseline (49%)**; participants **preferred and trusted the
less effective designs**; and high Need-for-Cognition users benefited disproportionately —
**intervention-generated inequality.**

## THE FOUR PLACES YOU CAN ACTUALLY CATCH A FAST EXPERT

1. **Orientation, before the situation** — commander's intent, premortem cue-priming, scenario
   practice that expands the pattern repertoire. Boyd, Moltke and Klein all point here.
   (Premortem's real payoff, from Klein's own HBR piece and almost never quoted: it *"sensitizes
   the team to pick up early signs of trouble once the project gets under way"* — perceptual
   priming for later fast recognition, an upstream intervention wearing a deliberation costume.)
2. **A pre-committed trigger on an external observable.** LCES, ALPTRUTh thresholds, the
   10,000-ft sterile-cockpit rule, implementation intentions (Gollwitzer & Sheeran: 94 tests,
   >8,000 participants, **d = .65**). **The trigger must NEVER be an internal feeling** — no
   valid internal signal exists. It must be a state of the world with a truth value a tired
   person can check now.
3. **Expectancy violation** — the self-correcting element already inside the RPD loop.
4. **Feedback, downstream** — the AAR *with an objective record*. The only intervention here
   with two converging meta-analyses (Tannenbaum & Cerasoli: 46 samples, N=2,136, ~25%
   improvement, **d = .67**). It changes the NEXT fast judgement, not the current one.

**The negative rule, from Weick and Putnam:** *never install a trigger whose response is
socially costly.* A trigger that names a hazard without supplying a permitted, practised
response **makes the hazard harder to see, not easier** (Westrum via Weick: capacity to act
gates capacity to see — paediatricians could not see battered-child syndrome until social
workers gave them something to do about it).

## Field evidence: the traps are social, and they invert with skill

McCammon, 715 US avalanche accidents 1972–2003, 1,355 individuals, 504 deaths.
**Almost three-quarters occurred with three or more OBVIOUS hazard indicators present.**

- **Exposure scores did not significantly decrease with training** across four levels.
- **Familiarity × training:** advanced-trained parties in familiar terrain exposed themselves to
  about the same hazard as untrained parties (p < 0.0001).
- **Social facilitation is bidirectional and keyed to self-assessed competence:** untrained
  groups became *more* cautious when observed; **trained groups became significantly LESS
  cautious** (p < 0.0001). **Being watched amplifies whatever you already believe about your own
  skill.**
- **Expert halo:** untrained parties led by an *unskilled* leader took more risk than untrained
  leaderless parties deciding by consensus (p = 0.0002).

His design conclusion, which is the practical heart of this whole round:

> Teaching about human factors alone probably won't significantly reduce accidents. The
> challenge is to **encode knowledge into simple, easily-applied decision tools that can COMPETE
> with the heuristic traps** — they don't need to be perfect, just more accurate than the social
> cues victims rely on.

And the design signature of the tools built on it (Obvious Clues / Avaluator): they **do not
estimate risk and are explicitly not go/no-go aids.** They identify **when you have entered a
situation where your decisions have become critical.** Functionally identical to LCES: not a
verdict — **a trigger telling you your judgement has just become load-bearing.**

## Weick: the failure is pre-deliberative

Mann Gulch — the crew did not face "where should we go"; they faced the discovery that **their
old labels had stopped working**, and until they made sense of that there was *nothing to
decide*. A **cosmology episode** is when the sense of what is occurring *and the means to
rebuild that sense* collapse together. Trigger: a false pattern match ("this is a 10:00 fire" —
correct on every previous fire). Structural: *if your job is to put out fires before they get
big, you never learn about big fires.*

**Under pressure people regress to their most habituated response** — so improvisation must
itself be habituated or it is not available.

**"Drop your tools"**: ≥23 wildland firefighters died since 1990 with tools beside them, within
sight of reachable safety. Reasons include **pluralistic ignorance** (if one keeps his tools,
others infer he isn't scared — *the situation appears safe though no one believes it is*),
**no skill at dropping**, and **identity fusion** (told to drop his tools, a firefighter is told
to forget he is a firefighter).

**Putnam's incentive analysis, and it is not moral:** if a stigma attaches to deploying a
shelter, people take more risk to escape; if a stigma attaches to dropping packs, they carry
everything while outrunning fire.

**How a memorised list became a blame instrument:** the 1957 Fire Orders carried the subscript
"follow each order *when it applies*." The **1987 revision reordered them to spell
F-I-R-E O-R-D-E-R-S for memorisation**, shifting emphasis to discrete items all to be followed
at once. Then: South Canyon 1994, 8 of 10 "violated"; Thirtymile 2001, all 10 broken.
⚠️ **Do not cite "the Orders were violated in nearly every fatality fire" as an independent
finding** — the Orders were the *investigative instrument*. Finding violations was guaranteed.
Putnam's inversion: if they are consistently found violated, perhaps they are impossible to
follow.

## Speed is not the enemy

**Burns 2004** (chess): *"up to 81% of variance in chess skill was accounted for by how players
performed with less than 5% of normal time available."* Remove 95% of the time, remove ~19% of
the skill signal.

**Sherbino 2012** (75 medical graduates, 25 cases): **r = −0.54** — faster diagnoses were *more*
accurate. Within-expert, speed marks successful recognition.

**But bidirectional:** ALQahtani 2016, 42 senior residents, accuracy 0.33 under time pressure vs
0.51 control (p=.012) — though the manipulation (repeated admonishment of falling behind)
plausibly induced anxiety as much as time pressure.

**Deliberation can actively hurt** (Wilson & Schooler): analysing reasons made students agree
*less* with experts, by focusing attention on non-optimal criteria that then drove the choice.

## Observation and accountability — directly relevant to "the operator is waiting"

**Choking is explicit monitoring, not distraction** (Beilock & Carr): experts showed
**impoverished episodic recollection of their own putts** — skilled performance is encoded
procedurally. Choking was **unchanged by dual-task training but eliminated by
self-consciousness training**. Self-focus helps novices and hurts experts.

**Bond & Titus**, 241 studies, ~24,000 subjects: presence of others **impairs complex-task
accuracy**, slightly facilitates simple-task accuracy. Effects are small (0.3–3% of variance)
and — the popular version drops this — **surprisingly unrelated to evaluation apprehension.**

> Applied: observation degrades the *novel, effortful, non-dominant* response and leaves the
> *well-learned dominant* one intact. An expert doing something familiar under observation is
> fine. An expert doing something genuinely novel under observation is in the impaired condition
> — **and their felt confidence will not distinguish the two, because it is generated by the
> same recognition machinery either way.**

**Accountability, and the timing result is the whole game** (Lerner & Tetlock): accountability to
an audience whose views are unknown, imposed **BEFORE** a judgement is formed, produces
preemptive self-criticism and more integratively complex thought. Imposed **AFTER** the judgement
exists, it produces **defensive bolstering**. **Process** accountability beats **outcome**
accountability. And accountability can *amplify* bias (Tetlock & Boettger: accountable subjects
procrastinated and buckpassed despite evidence more lives would be saved than lost).

> **Reviewing an expert's decision after they have stated it does not produce revision — it
> produces a better-articulated defence of the original answer.** Corroborated by Monteiro's 8%.

## Contested / unverified (preserved from the arm)

- Klein's premortem "30%" describes the **number of reasons generated**, not their correctness.
- "Training makes avalanche users worse" is **not established** — accident-only data, no denominator.
- Avalanche tool "60–92%" figures are **prevention values on accident data, not accuracy**.
- "Fire Orders violated in nearly every fatality fire" — artefact of the investigative instrument.
- Keiser & Arthur 2021 effect size: sources gave both 0.67 and 0.79 — verify before quoting.
- Tetlock 1983 before/after timing: full text unretrievable; stated as literature consensus.
- Weick 1993 contains a propagated factual error (post-Mann-Gulch fatalities; Loop Fire 1966).
- Boyd never made a quantitative claim about skipping Decide — **that number is Klein's.**
